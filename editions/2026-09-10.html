August took the corpus international.

feature
Three countries in one month

At the end of July the Axiom Foundation covered US federal and state law and UK council schemes. A month later three more countries are in, and the shape of the work did not change to accommodate them.

Canada came first, with the Canada Workers Benefit as administered by the CRA. Belgium followed with a worker personal-income-tax pipeline, and Denmark after that. Denmark taught us something: Danish law does not publish itself the way US law does, so the pipeline had to learn Retsinformation’s LexDania format before it could read a single statute.

That is the part worth dwelling on. Every country writes its benefits law differently and publishes it differently again — but the step that comes next is identical everywhere. Somebody translates prose into something a machine can run, privately, from scratch, for one system at a time. Three countries in one month suggests that step generalises better than it looks like it should.

product
Product updates

The corpus · 28 August 2026
9,069 source documents · 94,366 provisions · 4,155 RuleSpec encodings
Every figure is live on the coverage page — check them against this email whenever you like.

At launch on 28 July we published just over 3,000 encodings, so the corpus grew by roughly a third in a month. The US picture stays uneven and we would rather show that than average it away — Colorado leads the states at 1,010 encodings, North Carolina follows at 489 and California at 389, while a dozen states sit in single digits. Breadth of source documents came first; encoding depth follows program by program.

On the verification side, the validation dashboard became the cutover process this month — four measured gates a package has to clear rather than a judgment call someone makes. We also added guards against silent failure, so a package that quietly stops covering what it claims gets caught by the system rather than by a reader. Being open is not the differentiating part; being checkable is.

The API also learned to answer questions about time. You can now ask what a rule computed on a date in the past rather than only what it computes today — for anyone reconstructing a determination, whether that is an appeal, an audit, or a researcher checking a paper, that difference is the whole job. The same release lets a caller ask for one value by name instead of pulling a whole household result apart.

If you work on benefits administration in a state sitting in single digits, reply and name the program that would matter most. We would rather that list be shaped by people who work in these programs than by what happens to be easiest to reach.
